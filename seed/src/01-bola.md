# Interview 1: Bola, buka owner in Surulere

Interviewer: Thanks for making time, Aunty Bola. I know the lunch rush just ended. Can you tell me a bit about the restaurant?
Bola: No problem at all, my dear. It is called Mama Bola's Kitchen. I started it nine years ago with two pots and one gas cooker. Now I have six staff, and we serve about sixty plates on a good day. Amala, ewedu, rice and stew, pepper soup on weekends. Mostly office people from the area and some students.
Interviewer: That's impressive. So, I'm working on a mobile app for restaurant owners. Before I say anything about it, can you walk me through how you keep track of your stock today? Things like rice, tomatoes, palm oil, meat.
Bola: Ah, okay. {{pb0|Describes a concrete, current manual routine; shows a process exists, not that she would pay to replace it.|Every night when we close, I write down what came in and what is left in a big notebook.}} {{cp+|Quantifies the time cost of the manual process: an hour every night.|My younger sister Kemi sits with me and we check it against the receipts, and it takes about an hour.}} Then in the morning the cook tells me what is finished and I buy again at the market.
Interviewer: And does that notebook always match what is on the shelf?
Bola: Not always. {{cp+|A specific, recent incident: stock-out on a busy day, linked to the notebook process.|Last month we ran out of rice on a Friday afternoon, and Friday is my best day.}} The notebook said we had two bags. When we checked, one bag had been used for a naming ceremony order that nobody wrote down. So I had to send the boy to buy rice at the retail price, and some customers had already left.
Interviewer: How much did that cost you, roughly?
Bola: I did not calculate it. Maybe the plates we lost, and the extra money for the rice. It is not a thing I can say exactly.
Interviewer: Okay. {{lq|It pitches the solution inside the question and invites agreement instead of asking what she does today.|Tell me about the last time you ran out of something you needed. What happened, and what did you do next?|Wouldn't it be great if an app counted your stock for you, so you never ran out on a Friday again?}}
Bola: {{cm0|Agreement with a leading question, with no behavior behind it.|Oh, that would be fantastic.}} {{fp+|A promise prompted by the interviewer's pitch; weak evidence.|I would definitely pay for something like that.}} {{cm0|Praise with nothing attached.|That is a brilliant idea, my dear.}}
Interviewer: How much would you pay, do you think?
Bola: Haha, you are asking me price now. Let me see. {{fp+|A hypothetical price offered on the spot, no commitment.|If it is not too costly, I can pay two or three thousand naira a month.}} But I would have to see it first. These things sometimes look nice on the phone and then nobody uses them.
Interviewer: Have you used any app for the business before?
Bola: Only the bank app, and WhatsApp. I get my orders on WhatsApp. {{pb0|Names the tools actually used; none are for inventory.|We have never used a special app for the kitchen or the stock.}} My nephew tried to set up something for me once, but I did not have time for it.
Interviewer: What happened with that?
Bola: Nothing. He put it on my phone, I opened it two times and I forgot. The shop was too busy that month.
Interviewer: Who would use the app in your place? You, or the staff?
Bola: Me, mostly. The cook, Mama Ngozi, does not like phones. She is sixty-two. {{og0|A general statement about staff attitudes, not a measured behavior.|The staff can learn if you teach them slowly.}} But I would be the one entering things.
Interviewer: And would you enter the deliveries every time they come in?
Bola: {{fp+|Promises future diligence; contradicts her own admission about the earlier app.|Yes, I would enter everything, every single day.}} Because it would save me the hour I spend with Kemi at night. Actually, I want that hour back, because Kemi has her own work.
Interviewer: When you say you want that hour back, what would you do with it?
Bola: Rest, my dear. Or prepare for the next day. I wake up at four in the morning.
Interviewer: Let me ask about money. How do you pay your suppliers?
Bola: Mostly cash, some transfer. {{pb0|A neutral description of existing payment habits.|The rice woman and the meat seller, I pay them on Saturdays for the whole week.}} The others I pay on the spot.
Interviewer: What do you spend on your phone every month, data and so on?
Bola: About three thousand naira on data. I use it for orders and for my daughter in Canada. So if the app is another three thousand, hmm, that is double. I must be sure.
Interviewer: That's helpful to know. Do other owners you know have the same problem with stock?
Bola: {{og+|Generalizes about other owners without naming a specific case.|I think most buka owners are suffering the same way, because everybody uses notebook.}} Some of them are even worse than me. They keep it in their head.
Interviewer: So let me understand, you would use this and you would pay for it?
Bola: {{fp+|Another promise in response to a direct yes/no question.|Yes, I will use it, and I will tell my friends in the market association about it.}} {{cm0|Praise, no behavior.|You are doing a good thing, young man.}}
Interviewer: Is there anything that would stop you from using it?
Bola: Network, maybe. Sometimes the network disappears in the afternoon. And light. If there is no light I cannot charge my phone. But that is Lagos, we manage.
Interviewer: Last question. If I came back in two months with a working version, would you try it?
Bola: Of course. {{fp+|Says she will try it; unlike a purchase or a trial, it costs her nothing to say.|Bring it, I will try it, you can even come and sit in the shop.}} And bring the price list.
Interviewer: Thank you so much, Aunty Bola. I really appreciate your time.
Bola: You are welcome. {{cm0|Closing encouragement with no evidence content.|God will bless your work.}} Go and eat something before you go, there is rice in the pot.
