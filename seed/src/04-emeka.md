# Interview 4: Emeka, mama put owner in Lekki

Interviewer: Emeka, thank you for the time. I know you are on your feet all day. What's the setup here?
Emeka: No problem. This place is called Emeka's Table, but everyone calls it Mama Put, because my mother started it. We are in Lekki Phase One, behind the plaza. Twenty-eight seats, and we do a lot of delivery orders on the food apps. I took it over from my mother four years ago when she got sick. She is better now but she only comes to cook the soups on Saturdays.
Interviewer: How many people work with you?
Emeka: Nine. Three cooks, two kitchen assistants, three servers, and one rider for our own deliveries.
Interviewer: I'm working on an app for restaurant inventory. {{lq|It presupposes that tracking by hand is a nightmare and signals the answer the founder wants.|Walk me through how you keep track of stock today.|Surely tracking stock by hand is a nightmare for you?}}
Emeka: {{cm0|Agrees politely with the leading framing; says nothing about his own practice.|Ah, you have said it very well.}} {{og0|A broad statement about the restaurant trade, not about his own behavior.|Everybody in this business knows that stock is a headache.}}
Interviewer: How do you track yours right now?
Emeka: {{pb-|Describes a concrete attempt to use a tool that he abandoned after three weeks.|Last year I built a spreadsheet for the store, with all the items and the prices, and I used it for three weeks and then I stopped.}} I think it was too much typing. After service I am tired, and the cooks do not want to wait.
Interviewer: What happened to the stock after you stopped?
Emeka: We went back to the old way, which is looking at the shelf and asking the kitchen assistants. {{pb0|A neutral description of the fallback routine.|My kitchen assistant, Samuel, tells me every evening what is low, and I order the next day.}}
Interviewer: How often do things run out?
Emeka: Not too often. {{cp0|Minor, infrequent: the owner does not describe a costly problem.|Maybe once or twice a month something finishes, like crayfish or egusi, and I send the rider to the market.}} It is annoying but it is not a disaster.
Interviewer: So what would change if you had the app?
Emeka: {{cm0|Generic enthusiasm.|I love the idea.}} {{cm0|More generic enthusiasm.|It is very modern, and I like modern things.}} {{fp+|A promise with no price, timeline, or trial attached.|I will definitely download it when it is ready.}}
Interviewer: And would you pay for it?
Emeka: {{fp+|Another soft promise with a conditional.|I would pay if it is something that really helps, yes.}} But you know, I already pay for so many things. The food apps take twenty-five percent of every order, and the POS has its own monthly fee.
Interviewer: Tell me about the food apps. How do you manage orders from them?
Emeka: {{pb0|Concrete detail about delivery platforms and what he is already paying.|They send the order to a tablet, and my server confirms it, and then the kitchen prepares it.}} Sometimes a customer orders something we already finished, and we have to cancel, and they give us a bad rating. That is actually the thing that hurts me more than the stock.
Interviewer: Say more about that.
Emeka: When an item is finished, I have to go to the tablet and switch it off. {{cp+|A specific, recurring pain, closer to stock visibility than anything else he says.|If I forget, we get three or four cancelled orders and the app reduces my ranking.}} It happened twice in December.
Interviewer: So if the app knew what was in stock, it could switch items off for you?
Emeka: That would be very nice. {{fp+|A hypothetical reaction to a feature idea; no commitment.|If it can connect to the food app, I would pay good money for it.}} But I do not think those apps allow other apps to connect to them.
Interviewer: Okay. Do other owners around Lekki have the same problems?
Emeka: {{og+|Claims about others without a specific example.|I believe most restaurants in Lekki don't track their stock at all.}} They are just guessing. Some of them are doing well anyway.
Interviewer: And what about you? Do you think you need better tracking?
Emeka: {{og0|Self-assessment with no supporting behavior.|I think I could do better, but I am not sure that I need an app for it.}} If I had more time, maybe I would sit down and fix the spreadsheet.
Interviewer: What did the spreadsheet look like? What did you put in it?
Emeka: {{pb0|Describes the structure of the abandoned spreadsheet in concrete terms.|I had one column for the item, one for the quantity, one for the price, and one for the date I bought it.}} I even made it colourful. The problem was that every delivery needed me to open the laptop, and the laptop stays at home.
Interviewer: So the laptop was the obstacle.
Emeka: Partly. The bigger thing is that the numbers were never right the next day. {{cp0|Explains why his past tracking attempt lost credibility, without a cost attached.|Samuel would use something from the store at night and not tell me, so my sheet said we had more than we really had.}} After two or three days I stopped believing the sheet, and then I stopped opening it.
Interviewer: How did you feel when you stopped?
Emeka: Relieved, if I am honest. {{og0|A general feeling about recording stock, offered as opinion.|When you track stock, it feels like you are always behind.}} Now I just walk in and look.
Interviewer: Let me ask about the delivery apps again. How much of your revenue comes through them?
Emeka: {{pb0|Concrete figure on the revenue mix, useful context for the product.|About a third of our sales come from the food apps now, and it keeps growing.}} That is why the cancelled orders worry me. Every cancelled order is money and ranking.
Interviewer: Would you check an app on your phone every evening, as part of closing the shop?
Emeka: {{fp+|A promise about future routine; he already abandoned a similar habit.|Yes, if it is simple, I can do it before I close.}} But I told you, I said the same thing about the spreadsheet in the beginning.
Interviewer: Would you share your old spreadsheet with me? It would help me understand how you thought about it.
Emeka: Sure, I will send it. {{cm0|Courtesy.|You are very thorough, I like that.}}
Interviewer: One last thing. If you had to pick, what is the most annoying part of running the kitchen?
Emeka: Honestly? Staff turnover. {{cp0|A significant pain, but unrelated to inventory.|Every three months somebody leaves and I have to train another person from zero.}} So any app must be simple enough that a new person can learn it in one day.
Interviewer: That's a very useful constraint. Thank you, Emeka.
Emeka: My pleasure. Take some chin chin before you go.
