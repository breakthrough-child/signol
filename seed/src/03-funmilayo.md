# Interview 3: Funmilayo, owner of two small restaurants in Ikeja

Interviewer: Madam Funmilayo, thank you for having me. Tell me about your businesses.
Funmilayo: I have two places. The main restaurant is in Ikeja, near the airport road, forty seats. The second is a smaller food stall in the market, which my daughter manages. We started the restaurant eleven years ago. We sell Nigerian dishes, some continental for the office people, and I do small catering on weekends.
Interviewer: That's a lot to manage. How many staff do you have?
Funmilayo: Fourteen between the two places. Cooks, servers, a cleaner, a store keeper. The store keeper is the person who gets the supplies from the market and keeps the store.
Interviewer: Walk me through how stock is managed. From the moment the supplies arrive.
Funmilayo: Okay. {{pb0|A detailed, current manual process with a clear point of failure.|The store keeper receives the goods, writes it in the stock book, and the cook signs when he takes things out.}} In theory, that is how it works. {{pb+|A concrete recurring cost: she already pays a person monthly to reconcile stock records.|At the end of the month my cousin, who is an accountant, comes and reconciles the stock sheets, and I pay him fifteen thousand naira for that.}}
Interviewer: And does it reconcile? Do the numbers match?
Funmilayo: Ha! Never. {{cp+|A specific, quantified loss from stock mismatch, discovered in a recent month.|In March the books said we should have thirty kilos of beef, but the store had less than half, and the difference came to about one hundred and twenty thousand naira.}} We did not find out until the end of the month, when it was already gone.
Interviewer: What did you do when you found out?
Funmilayo: I was very angry. {{pb0|Describes a concrete action taken in response to the problem; a workaround rather than a purchase.|I changed the store keeper, and now I come to the store myself every Monday and Thursday to count the meat and the fish.}} But I cannot do that forever. I have two places and a family.
Interviewer: How much of your week does the counting take?
Funmilayo: Two mornings, about two hours each. {{cp+|Quantifies the personal time cost of the current workaround.|Those are four hours I should be using for catering orders.}}
Interviewer: {{lq|It asks for a future promise and ends with 'right?' to invite agreement.|If a tool for this existed today, what would you have to see before you changed how your store keeper works?|You'd definitely use an app that fixes that, right?}}
Funmilayo: {{fp+|A promise elicited by a leading question; weak evidence by itself.|Yes, of course, I would use it.}} But the real question is whether my people will use it. The store keeper before could barely write properly.
Interviewer: What would you need from an app so the staff would use it?
Funmilayo: It must be very simple. {{og0|A product requirement stated as an opinion, not past behavior.|If it has too many buttons, they will not use it.}} Maybe pictures. And it must work when there is no data, because the store has bad network.
Interviewer: Have you tried anything like this before?
Funmilayo: {{pb-|A concrete past attempt that failed because of staff adoption.|Two years ago I bought a small barcode scanner for the store, but the store keeper never used it, and it is still in a drawer.}} Maybe I did not train them well. That is my own fault.
Interviewer: How much did that scanner cost?
Funmilayo: About thirty-five thousand naira, with the software it came with. It was a waste. So I am careful now.
Interviewer: Would you pay a monthly fee for something that solved the problem?
Funmilayo: {{fp+|A conditional promise tied to price, with a ceiling she names.|If it really stops the theft, I can pay up to ten thousand naira a month.}} Because I already pay my cousin fifteen. If the app replaces him, it is a saving.
Interviewer: Who else would you want to see the numbers?
Funmilayo: My daughter, at the stall. And maybe my husband, he likes to check everything. {{pb0|A neutral detail about how decisions are made in the household business.|He reads every report I give him, twice.}}
Interviewer: Is there anything else about running two places that is hard?
Funmilayo: Cash. And staff. And gas. {{og0|A general complaint about running a restaurant.|Everything in this business is hard, my dear.}} But the stock thing is the one that costs me money without me seeing it.
Interviewer: Tell me about the last time the numbers surprised you.
Funmilayo: Just last week, actually. {{cp+|A recent, specific discovery of loss, found through manual counting.|When I counted on Thursday I found that eight crates of eggs were missing from what the book said, and nobody could explain it.}} The store keeper said maybe the cook used them for the catering order, but the catering order only needed two crates. So I do not know where six went.
Interviewer: What did you do about it?
Funmilayo: I put a padlock on the store and I keep one key. {{pb+|Concrete protective action: a padlock and a single key held by her.|Now the cook has to call me before he can take anything out, even on Sunday during church.}} It is stressful for everybody, I know. But I cannot lose that kind of money.
Interviewer: How does the stall in the market compare?
Funmilayo: My daughter is more careful, because it is smaller, and she counts every evening. {{pb0|Describes her daughter's nightly counting routine, a working manual process.|She writes it in a school exercise book and sends me a picture on WhatsApp.}} I check the picture when I have time. But at the restaurant it is too much.
Interviewer: And what do you do with the picture?
Funmilayo: Nothing, to be honest. I look at it and say, okay, fine. If there is a big shortage she calls me, if not, we move.
Interviewer: When you talk to other restaurant owners, do they have the same problem?
Funmilayo: {{og+|Claims other owners share the problem; hearsay rather than evidence.|They all complain about the same thing at the association meeting, so I think it is common.}} Nobody wants to say how much they lose, though.
