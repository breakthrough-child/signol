# Interview 5: Tunde, owner of a long-running buka in Mushin

Interviewer: Mr Alabi, thank you for letting me come. Please tell me about the place.
Tunde: Call me Tunde. This place is called Alabi's Pot, and I have been here fifteen years. Before I had a stall at the bus stop, then I rented this shop. We do amala, ewedu, gbegiri, pounded yam, assorted meat. Thirty plates is a slow day, sixty on a market day. My wife started with me, now she runs the kitchen and I run the front and the money.
Interviewer: How many people work here?
Tunde: Seven. Two of them have been with me for ten years. The others come and go.
Interviewer: I'm building a mobile app to help restaurant owners keep track of their stock. Before I talk about it, can you tell me how you manage your stock now?
Tunde: {{pb-|A concrete, current routine that works for him and the head cook; no tool involved.|Every morning at six, before the fire is lit, my wife and I walk through the store and count the main things with our eyes, the yam, the flour, the oil, the meat in the freezer, and we write the shopping list on the back of the previous day's receipt.}} It takes fifteen minutes.
Interviewer: And how often do you run out of things?
Tunde: {{pb-|A strong, specific outcome that contradicts the existence of the problem.|In the last two years I have not run out of anything important on a trading day, not once.}} Maybe small things like pepper, once or twice. But that is not a thing to talk about.
Interviewer: Okay. And what about theft or waste? Do you track losses?
Tunde: The staff who have been with me ten years don't steal, and I know the new ones will try, so I watch them. {{cp0|Says loss is controlled by supervision, not by a system.|I stand in the front and I count the plates that go out, and at the end of the day the money must match the plates.}} Anything else is not necessary.
Interviewer: Have you tried any tools or apps for the business?
Tunde: {{pb-|A concrete failed attempt: he tried an inventory app and abandoned it after a week.|Last year a young man came with a stock app, he sold it to me for four thousand naira for the year, I installed it, I used it for one week and then I deleted it.}}
Interviewer: What happened in that week?
Tunde: Everything we did in fifteen minutes in the morning now took one hour. {{pb-|Quantifies why the tool made things worse: more time than the manual routine.|I had to type every bag of flour, every tomato, every little thing, and my wife could not read the small letters on the phone.}} My wife said, Tunde, are we now working for the phone or is the phone working for us?
Interviewer: That's really useful. What did you do with the four thousand naira?
Tunde: I lost it. Business is business, you cannot cry for four thousand naira. But I learned.
Interviewer: If an app could do all of that automatically, without any typing, how would you feel about it?
Tunde: It is a nice idea. {{cm0|Polite praise for the concept in general.|It is a nice idea for the big hotels and the places that have manager.}} But I am not a hotel.
Interviewer: Would you pay for it?
Tunde: {{fp-|A clear refusal to pay, stated with a price, which is rare and valuable.|No. I would not pay for it. Not five thousand a month, not two thousand.}} {{og-|States his general view on paying monthly for apps.|I pay my suppliers, my light bill, my generator fuel, my rent, I don't pay apps.}}
Interviewer: What would have to be true for you to change your mind?
Tunde: {{fp-|Names a hypothetical condition, but still refuses a monthly fee.|If it was free and it did everything by itself, I would look at it, but I still would not pay monthly.}} Because next year somebody comes with another one.
Interviewer: Tell me about a time when you did pay for a tool or service that helped the business.
Tunde: {{pb0|A concrete example of what he does pay for: a physical, one-off purchase.|I bought a big deep freezer three years ago for four hundred and fifty thousand naira, because without it the meat was spoiling.}} That one I could see. I put meat in it, I take meat out.
Interviewer: So you pay for things that solve a problem you can see.
Tunde: Exactly. {{og-|A principle that frames inventory software as an invisible benefit.|If I cannot see the problem, I will not pay for the solution.}}
Interviewer: What happens on a day when your wife is not around?
Tunde: {{pb-|A concrete example of the process continuing without one person; the routine is robust.|When she travelled for her sister's wedding last year, the senior cook did the morning count with me, and we did not miss a single item for the whole week.}} The routine is in our hands now. It is not in a phone.
Interviewer: And when the price of things changes, how do you find out?
Tunde: {{pb0|Describes how he learns about prices: directly from sellers, in person.|I go to the market twice a week myself, and the women there tell me the new price before I ask.}} I have known some of them for twenty years.
Interviewer: Do you think other owners would pay?
Tunde: {{og0|Speculation about others, offered as a hunch.|Maybe the ones who have many branches, I don't know.}} I only know about myself. The buka owners I know are like me: the person who owns the place is there every day. If you are not there every day, the place will fall.
Interviewer: Thank you for being so direct. This is exactly what I need to hear.
Tunde: You are welcome. {{cm0|Mild encouragement, not a commitment.|Maybe you should build it for the big restaurants, not for us.}}
Interviewer: Is there anything else you want to tell me?
Tunde: Only that you should go and sit in the shops. Don't sit in an office and build things. See how we do it, and you will see that sometimes the old way is fine.
Interviewer: I'll do that. Thanks again, Tunde.
Tunde: Go well. And don't leave without eating.
