Interviewer: Ngozi, thanks for fitting me in. I know your lunch crowd is coming soon. Tell me about the business.
Ngozi: Sure, I have about twenty minutes. I run a thirty-seat restaurant on Victoria Island, mostly office workers from the banks around here, and a catering arm that does office lunches and events. We have been open for six years. We started with five staff, now we are at fifteen. The catering side is about forty percent of our revenue.
Interviewer: That's a big operation for a small team. How do you manage inventory across the restaurant and the catering?
Ngozi: With difficulty, honestly. We have a shared WhatsApp group with the kitchen manager, the chef and me, and every evening the chef posts a photo of the store shelves. Then I look at the picture and say what to buy. Sometimes the picture is blurry.
Interviewer: Do you have any software for the business at all?
Ngozi: Yes. We pay eight thousand naira every month for a POS system, which handles sales, receipts and the daily report. It has been about eighteen months. It also came with a stock module.
Interviewer: And do you use the stock module?
Ngozi: No. We bought it and the stock part has been empty since the first week, because nobody has time to enter the deliveries. The chef says he is a chef, not a data entry person. And I agree with him.
Interviewer: So what would you want from a different tool?
Ngozi: Something that doesn't need people to type. If a tool needs my staff to do extra work, it will die in a month. I have seen it happen with three different things.
Interviewer: Wouldn't it be amazing if a tool could do all the counting for you?
Ngozi: It would be nice, yes. It sounds like a really good concept.
Interviewer: How much does the lack of visibility cost you?
Ngozi: I have not measured it. What I know is that last quarter we had to substitute a protein on two catering orders because the store was short, and one client complained. It's the kind of thing that damages reputation.
Interviewer: What did you do after the complaint?
Ngozi: I apologised and gave a discount on their next order. That discount was about twenty percent of a hundred and eighty thousand naira order. Then I told the chef to count the freezer more carefully, and that lasted about two weeks.
Interviewer: Would you pay for something that fixed the stock visibility?
Ngozi: Probably, if the price makes sense and it doesn't need extra work. But I want to see it work with my own stock first.
Interviewer: Do you have a number in mind?
Ngozi: I would pay maybe ten to fifteen thousand a month if it saved us from even one bad order. But I want a trial, not a contract.
Interviewer: Okay. And who makes decisions on tools like this, you or someone else?
Ngozi: Me, with my operations manager. We decided on the POS together after watching three demos and testing one for a month. I do not buy things quickly.
Interviewer: What was it about the POS that convinced you?
Ngozi: The support. When the receipt printer failed on a Friday, they sent someone within two hours, and I remember that. For a tool like yours, that matters more than features.
Interviewer: How do you handle ordering for a big catering event, say a hundred people?
Ngozi: We plan it on paper three days before. The chef writes the menu, I convert it to quantities in a spreadsheet, and the kitchen manager goes to the market the day before with cash. It works when the numbers are small. For events, one mistake is expensive.
Interviewer: Has there been a mistake recently?
Ngozi: A few months ago we under-ordered chicken for a wedding of one hundred and twenty guests. We had to send a rider to two different markets at six in the morning, and the chicken cost us almost double the price. The client never knew, but I did.
Interviewer: And how did you feel about the cost?
Ngozi: Annoyed with myself. It was not the app's fault, nobody's fault. It was a planning mistake on my side, and a tool would not have fixed it alone. I'd say it is the combination of a good process and good tools.
Interviewer: Do you think your staff would accept a new tool?
Ngozi: The younger ones, yes. The older cooks sometimes resist anything new, but they come around if they see it saves their time. The kitchen manager is the key person. If he likes it, everyone will follow.
Interviewer: That's a helpful point. Anything else you'd want me to know?
Ngozi: I would say talk to my chef. You seem to ask the right questions. He understands the store better than I do.
Interviewer: I'd love that. Thanks so much, Ngozi.
Ngozi: Anytime. Here is his number.
